# Make key information dominant

Decide what the user or reader should see first and what they should be able to do next. Spend the strongest type, colour, and space on those two things. Put them in the first view.

- Cut everything including titles, intros, section heading, group box title, and card labels if it merely names the obvious thing next to it. Do not give each widget or component its own heading, subtitle, and caption. A website may have a header, but it shouldn't take up vertical space by itself. Most websites have their logo and name to the side, in a side panel, or alongside the search or directory.
- Put auxiliary information such as units beside the corresponding value, and a control beside the result it changes. Don't add extra linebreaks or paragraphs for things that logically live in one line.
- Design the layout of the main canvas, key results, interaction controls, charts, visualizations etc before worrying about adding labels, subheadings, etc.
- Do not label things that are self-explanatory. Use short terms or phrases where sufficient.
- Reserve extended prose for where details are not obvious to the user, e.g. "This data has not been reviewed, so may be incorrect", "This includes <subcategory>, see <link> for <specific case>". Keep caveats that materially change the interpretation beside the affected result.
- Expose a useful control for every primary task by default.
- Avoid dropdowns for cases where you only have a few, known options. It's quicker to click buttons. Use sliders plus numerical boxes where appropriate.

For example, lead with two large comparison charts. Put the comparison choices above the chart they control, write the annual unit beside each total, and use a single shared legend.
